Heat-Ready Schools sprint · school heat safety, measured

Your hottest term, measured. Your pupils, ready for it.

The Heat-Ready Schools sprint puts instruments in your classrooms and playground for one hot term, gives your principal a signed heat protocol, and hands the data to your pupils as a project. You finish with a School Heat Report parents and boards can read.

Who this is for

Schools that would rather know than guess.

  • Private schools and school groups, CBSE, ICSE and state board
  • Principals and heads who answer to parents on every hot day
  • CSR committees funding government and aided schools
  • Trusts and foundations that run or support schools
The problem, plainly

Heat reaches the classroom before it reaches the timetable.

UNICEF counted at least 242 million students whose schooling was disrupted by climate events in 2024. Heatwaves accounted for 171 million of them.

Source: UNICEF, Learning Interrupted, January 2025

States are answering with rules: adjust school hours, avoid outdoor activity at peak heat. A few cities are piloting cool roofs. What almost no school has is a reading from inside its own classrooms. Without it, every decision on a hot day is a guess, and every request for shade or a cool roof is an opinion.

A school that measures its own heat, teaches its pupils to read it and acts on a written protocol is safer this summer. It also becomes the school that can show its parents and its board what it did.

  1. 01 / 08

    The school as it is today

    Timetables shift when the heat order comes. Assembly gets shorter. Sport gets cancelled. Nobody knows how hot the top-floor classrooms run at two in the afternoon, or which pupils stop coming when it does.

    What goes on recordNothing yet. Most schools have never taken a reading inside a classroom.
  2. 02 / 08

    We walk the school with the principal

    One morning. The hottest block, usually the top floor under a concrete roof. The playground. The corridor where pupils queue. We agree where instruments go and which classes will run the modules.

    What goes on recordA school code, a plan of the building, and the instrument positions.
  3. 03 / 08

    Loggers in classrooms, an instrument in the playground

    A wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) point in the playground. Temperature and humidity loggers in three to six classrooms, chosen to compare the hottest rooms with the coolest. They log through the whole hot term, including the empty weeks, which show what the building does by itself.

    What goes on recordThe exposure series for the playground and every logged classroom.
  4. 04 / 08

    The principal signs a heat protocol

    One page, aligned to the state's heat action plan. What changes in each band: assembly, sport, water breaks, uniforms, the afternoon timetable. A named teacher or the school nurse owns the daily actions, and the band card goes up where pupils can read it.

    What goes on recordThe action log: what the school did on each alert day.
  5. 05 / 08

    Pupils take the readings

    Four project modules for classes 6 to 9, each ten to twelve hours, built to fit the CBSE skill-module slot. Pupils read the instruments, map the school's heat, learn what heat does to a body, audit the building and tell the story of the season in data. A teacher from your school runs them after a two-hour briefing.

    What goes on recordPupil projects: the school heat map, the building audit, the season's data story.
  6. 06 / 08

    A red-band day arrives

    The school already knows what to do. Sport moves to the early morning. The playground rests at midday. Water breaks are rung in. Parents get the alert-day message the evening before. Attendance that day is read against the band, so you see who stays home when it is hot.

    What goes on recordAttendance on alert days, incidents, and actions taken.
  7. 07 / 08

    Every week, one page for the principal

    The week's classroom and playground curves, the days in each band, what the school did, and one recommendation. Short enough to read between two meetings.

    What goes on recordA weekly readout through the hot term.
  8. 08 / 08

    The School Heat Report

    The season in one document parents, the board and the education office can follow: how hot the school ran, what it did, how attendance moved, what the pupils found, and the one or two building changes worth funding. It includes a section the pupils wrote.

    What goes on recordThe school's site-season in the register, and next year's baseline.
The four modules

Every module ends in a measurement or a design with a number in it.

Module 1 · classes 6 to 8

Reading the heat

Pupils take temperature, humidity and WBGT readings at six points around the school, plot a day's curve against the state's bands, and draw the heat map of their own school.

Module 2 · classes 6 to 9

The body in heat

What heat does to a body and why children heat up faster. The three grades of heat illness, first response, water and uniforms, and the buddy check.

Module 3 · classes 7 to 9

The school as a site

A heat audit of the building: roof, shade, ventilation, surfaces. Pupils measure surface temperatures and propose one costed change to the management.

Module 4 · classes 8 to 10

Telling the city

A season of readings becomes a data story. Pupils compare the school with the nearest weather station, read attendance on alert days, and present to parents or the education office.

Ten to twelve hours each, project-assessed, written to the CBSE skill-module shape for classes 6 to 8 and linked to science, geography and data science chapters.

School heat: questions a principal asks first

Straight answers before the call.

What is a Heat-Ready Schools sprint?

It is one hot term measured at your school. Loggers record classroom temperature and humidity and a playground instrument records wet-bulb globe temperature. The principal signs a one-page heat protocol. Pupils in classes 6 to 9 run four project modules on the school's own data. At the end the school receives a School Heat Report.

How do you protect pupils from heat at school?

By measuring first. The readings show which classrooms and which hours carry the risk. The protocol then moves sport, assembly and the afternoon timetable on alert days, sets water breaks, and tells parents the evening before. The report shows what worked and which building change, shade or a cool roof, would do the most.

Does this add to teachers' workload?

One teacher runs the modules after a two-hour briefing, inside the skill-module or club time the school already has. One named person owns the daily band card. We install the instruments, send the weekly readout and write the report.

Which classes take part, and does it fit the CBSE curriculum?

Classes 6 to 9. The four modules are written to the CBSE skill-module shape: school-based, project-assessed, ten to twelve hours each. They link to chapters pupils already study in science, geography and data science. A state-syllabus version follows the same plan.

Is any data collected about individual children?

No health screening is done on pupils. The register holds classroom readings, attendance counts on alert days and an incident log without names. Data terms are agreed in writing with the school before an instrument goes up.

Can a company fund this for government schools?

Yes. A CSR committee can fund a bundle of schools. The record maps to Schedule VII item (ii), education, and item (i), preventive health care, and the pack is written so the committee can assess the project.

What does the sprint cost?

A fixed figure per school, agreed in writing before the term starts. School groups and CSR-funded bundles are scoped together. Nothing is priced on this page because nothing is priced before a conversation.
