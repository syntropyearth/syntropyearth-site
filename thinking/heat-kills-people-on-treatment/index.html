Thinking · Evidence

Heat does not kill who you think. The people it kills are on treatment.

Abhijith Magal · 3 November 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

  • Europe's summer 2026 heat killed over 50,000 people across 854 cities, the most on record, with 32,619 deaths attributed to climate change. 22,502 of the dead, 45 per cent, were 85 or older. About 6,000 were under 65.
  • The Lancet Countdown's 2026 India data sheet values heat-related mortality among people over 65 at USD 33.8 billion a year, up 205 per cent on 2000 to 2004, equal to over 1.9 million average annual incomes.
  • A PLOS ONE study put men in their sixties in a 40 degree room for 90 minutes. Those with type 2 diabetes and hypertension raised their heart rate about 7 per cent against 22 per cent for healthy controls, and their baroreflex sensitivity fell about a quarter against more than half. Treated bodies respond less to heat, not more.
  • The heat-vulnerable person on a site is not the youngest labourer in the sun. It is the supervisor on blood pressure tablets, the driver with diabetes, the parent at home on three medicines. A roster that does not know who they are cannot protect them.

The picture most people carry of a heat death is a labourer collapsing on a building site at two in the afternoon. That death happens, and it is the one the newspapers find. It is not the typical one. When Imperial College London and the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine counted Europe's summer of 2026 across 854 cities, they found over 50,000 heat deaths between June and August, the highest on record, with 32,619 attributed to human-caused warming. Of those 50,000, 22,502 were people aged 85 or over. About 6,000 were under 65. The dead were old, at home, and most of them were on daily medicines for their heart, their kidneys or their blood sugar.

India does not have that count, a gap I wrote about in Europe counted its heat dead in season. What it has is a price. The Lancet Countdown's 2026 India data sheet values heat-related mortality among people over 65 at USD 33.8 billion a year, averaged over 2021 to 2025, a 205 per cent increase on 2000 to 2004 and equal to over 1.9 million average annual incomes. The age profile the Europeans measured is the one India has already monetised.

Why does heat hit a treated body differently?

A study published in PLOS ONE on 27 October 2026 gives the mechanism, in a small and careful way. Mojdeh Rafieian, Tiina Ikaheimo and colleagues at UiT The Arctic University of Norway and the University of Oulu, with Craig Crandall of Texas Health Presbyterian, put twenty men in their sixties through 90 minutes at 40 degrees and 50 per cent humidity: ten with type 2 diabetes and hypertension, ten age-matched healthy controls. The healthy men's heart rate rose by about 14 beats a minute, roughly 22 per cent, as their bodies pushed blood to the skin to shed heat. The men with diabetes and hypertension managed about 5 beats, roughly 7 per cent. Their mean baroreflex sensitivity, the reflex that steadies blood pressure when the body is stressed, fell by about 26 per cent against about 58 per cent in controls. Every measure of autonomic response moved less.

The authors' conclusion is one sentence: combined type 2 diabetes and hypertension produced blunted autonomic responses to short-term heat exposure, potentially increasing vulnerability to temperature extremes. Twenty men is a mechanism study, not a mortality estimate, and I will not stretch it further than that. It is the physiological line under the European count. The body that is already managing a chronic condition does not mount the heat response a healthy body does, and the medicines that manage the condition sit on top of that.

What else sets the risk, beyond the diagnosis?

A review in Age and Ageing, published 1 November 2026 by Laura Petraglia and colleagues at the University of Naples Federico II, argues that heat risk in older people is set by frailty, multimorbidity, housing and the ability to pay for cooling, together. It names "summer cooling poverty" as a distinct risk, shaped by energy prices, income, housing quality and health status together, not by whether a person owns an air conditioner, and it proposes a heat-adapted geriatric assessment that it says is not yet validated. The concept matters for India because the over-65 figure on the data sheet is a national average, and the person inside it who dies is the one who is frail, on several medicines, in a top-floor room with a tin roof, and unable to run a fan through the night. Age alone does not find that person. A list does.

What this means for a Heat-Safe Workers roster

Most heat protocols are written for the youngest body on the site: the loader, the mason, the man on the roof. They are right to be. But the evidence says the person most likely to be harmed on a 44 degree afternoon is the 52-year-old supervisor on two blood pressure tablets, the driver who manages his diabetes with a strip he sometimes skips, the security guard whose shift runs through the hottest hours of the day. Those people are on the roster. Almost no roster knows who they are, because nobody has asked, and the question sounds intrusive until the first collapse.

The annual health examination the OSHWC Central Rules of 8 May 2026 now require for workers over 40 in specified sectors is where the asking starts. The occupational physician already screens for blood pressure and blood sugar. Adding one line, whether the worker takes a daily medicine and for what, turns a compliance file into a heat risk register. From there the design is plain: those workers are checked by name when the warning goes red, their shifts are the first to move, and their refills are the thing the site counts at the end of the season. The Continuity Cohort Method does that counting, fixing the named group before the season and reconciling it after.

Then the second roster, the one no company holds: the workers' parents. Europe's dead were at home. India's over-65 heat mortality is valued at USD 33.8 billion a year. A worker whose mother is admitted with heat illness in May is absent in May, and a site that offers its workforce a simple heat check for the household's older members, through the same occupational health function, is protecting attendance as much as kindness. I measure what heat does to the people a company depends on, at the sites where they work and learn, and I build the programme the company pays for to change that number. The people a company depends on include the ones at home.

Heat protocols are written for the youngest body on the site. The evidence says heat finds the oldest and the most medicated, and most of them are at home.

Definition · climate-access

Climate-access is the gap between healthcare that exists on paper and healthcare people can actually reach once climate disruption is counted, read across four dimensions: availability, affordability, reachability and continuity. For a person on daily treatment, heat breaks continuity first, and continuity is what keeps them alive.

The fair caveats

Europe's figures are modelled from temperatures and mortality curves, not death certificates, and the authors exclude rural areas, so the toll is likely higher and the age split is for cities. The PLOS ONE study is twenty men in a laboratory and says so. The Age and Ageing paper is a conceptual review with a proposed assessment that has not been validated. And a US study of heart failure admissions among older Medicare patients, published in JAMA Network Open on 1 October, found cold, not heat, drove admissions in that population, which is a reminder to argue from access and continuity, where the evidence is strong, before physiology. None of this moves the operating point. A site that knows who on its roster takes a daily medicine can act on a red warning. One that does not is guessing.

Where this is headed

India will eventually produce the count Europe has, and when it does the age profile will be the same, because the physiology and the housing are the same. The sites that have already identified their treated workers, and the households behind them, will be able to show what they did in the season before the number arrived. It begins with the one question nobody has asked the roster: who here takes a tablet every day?
