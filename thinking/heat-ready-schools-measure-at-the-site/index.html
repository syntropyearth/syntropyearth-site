Thinking · Heat-Ready Schools

Measure heat where the child stands. WHO's new indicators, and the 2.7 degree gap.

Abhijith Magal · 3 November 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

  • On 21 October 2026 WHO, WMO, UNICEF and UNFPA published the first set of indicators for tracking what heat does to pregnant women, newborns and children. Nineteen indicators, six of them on policy. School attendance during extreme heat is one of the outcomes named.
  • A nine-day study at a public childcare centre in Aichi, Japan, found sun-exposed wet-bulb globe temperature at child height ran 2.7 degrees above the official published value. Shaded ground at 10 centimetres reached 42.8 degrees. The nearest weather station was 9.3 kilometres away.
  • Every heat decision a school makes, from closing the playground to sending a child home, is taken against a number measured somewhere else. The indicators now exist to report the right number. The reading has to come from the site.
  • A Heat-Ready Schools sprint is that reading, plus the attendance register, plus a plan a school trust can sign. None of it needs new equipment beyond a thermometer that reads what the child feels.

A school cannot act on a temperature measured nine kilometres away. That sentence is the whole argument, and two documents published in the same week of October 2026 make it unanswerable. The first is a set of indicators from four UN agencies that, for the first time, tells countries what to count when heat hits mothers and children. The second is a small Japanese study that measured heat where a toddler actually stands and found the official number was wrong by 2.7 degrees.

What did WHO publish on heat and children?

On 21 October WHO, the World Meteorological Organization, UNICEF and UNFPA released a 56-page publication titled "Priority indicators to monitor the impact of heat exposure on maternal, newborn and child health". It proposes core and additional indicators across four domains: routine maternal and child health monitoring, public health and surveillance systems, climate and health monitoring, and measures of heat exposure. Health Policy Watch, reporting the launch on 22 October, counted 19 indicators, six of them policy tests, one being whether a country's vulnerability assessment treats women and children as a distinct at-risk group.

The outcomes named are the ones a health system already records: preterm births, stillbirths, low birthweight, pregnancy hypertension, child deaths, and visits to a health facility during extreme heat. Then one that a health system does not record and a school does. School attendance. The agencies suggest tracking it to show how heat disrupts children's lives, which is a careful way of saying that a child who is not in class on a 44 degree day is a data point nobody has been collecting.

The numbers behind the release are the ones that will travel. Heat exposure is linked to a 26 per cent rise in the odds of preterm birth. Babies under one saw 389 per cent more heatwave days in 2024 than in the 1986 to 2005 baseline. About 1.5 billion children are exposed to heatwaves that are getting longer, more frequent or more severe. And from a WHO-commissioned review of 83 heat-health action plans across 24 countries: 52 per cent named pregnant women as an at-risk group. From a member-state survey cited by the International Confederation of Midwives: 80 per cent of countries mention heat in their national health adaptation plans, 30 per cent include concrete actions, and 2 per cent spell out actions for mothers, newborns and children.

What did the Japanese creche study find?

Saimi Yamamoto, Morihiro Ito and colleagues put wet-bulb globe thermometers at 0.5 metres and 0.1 metres, the heights of a standing and a crawling child, at a public childcare facility in Aichi Prefecture for nine days from 29 July to 8 August 2025. Readings every five minutes from 9am to 5pm, in sun, in shade and indoors. Then they compared each reading with the value the Ministry of the Environment publishes for the area, from a station 9.3 kilometres away.

In the sun, WBGT at 0.5 metres ran 2.7 degrees above the published figure, and 2.6 degrees above it at 0.1 metres. The gap widened as the day got hotter. The single highest reading of the study, 42.8 degrees WBGT, came not from the sun but from shaded ground at 10 centimetres, where heat radiating off the surface pooled at the height of a toddler on a mat. Indoors ran about 7 degrees below the official number. On 88.8 per cent of the ministry's own observations the day was already at a severe warning level or above.

Three things follow for anyone who runs a school. The official number understates the playground. The shade is not safe at ground level in the way an adult standing in it would assume. And the classroom may be cooler than the forecast implies, which means the right decision on a hot day is often not to close but to move, and only a site reading can tell you which.

Why does this matter for a school in India?

Because every heat decision an Indian school takes is already taken against a number from somewhere else. State governments close schools or shorten hours on the strength of an IMD district warning. A head teacher sends a class indoors on the strength of a phone forecast. Neither number was measured at that school, at that playground, at the height of that child. The Japanese study is the first I have seen that quantifies the error at a childcare site, and the error runs in the dangerous direction.

India does not lack heat action plans. It lacks the layer between the plan and the child. The 2 per cent figure describes national health adaptation plans worldwide, and the 52 per cent figure describes heat-health action plans that name pregnant women. The Indian version of the same gap is a state plan that says "schools shall remain closed" and a school that has no idea what its own courtyard measures at 11am in April. The WHO indicators give that school, for the first time, a list of what to report. Attendance during extreme heat. Facility visits during extreme heat. Whether a heat warning actually reached the building. Those are indicators a school trust can hold up to a CSR committee or a district education officer, and nobody in India is producing them yet.

What a Heat-Ready Schools sprint measures

The sprint is the reading, the register and the plan, in that order, and it takes weeks, not a school year. Thermometers at child height in the three places the Japanese team used: the sunlit playground, the shaded ground, the classroom. Readings through the hottest fortnight the calendar offers before the season starts in earnest. The attendance register laid over the readings, so the school can see for itself which days children stayed home and which days they came and sat in a room that was hotter than the forecast. The sick room log tied to the same dates.

Then the plan, written for that site. Which spaces are usable at which reading. When the playground closes. Where the water is. Which children carry a condition that heat worsens, from asthma to the medicines some of them take, and who checks on them. When the school tells parents, and through which channel, because a warning that reaches the district education office at 6am and the parent at noon has not reached the child. And who reports the indicators at the end of the season, so the next year starts from a number.

The official number is measured at adult height, at a station, in the open. The child is on a mat, in the shade, ten centimetres off the ground.

I measure what heat does to the people a company depends on, at the sites where they work and learn, and I build the programme the company pays for to change that number. For a school, the company is usually a CSR funder or an employer whose workers' children attend it, and the site is the courtyard. The measurement borrows nothing exotic. The WHO indicators are published. The thermometers are cheap. The register already exists. What has been missing is somebody standing in the courtyard at 11am with the right instrument, and a trust willing to put the result on paper.

Definition · climate-access

Climate-access is the gap between healthcare that exists on paper and healthcare people can actually reach once climate disruption is counted, read across four dimensions: availability, affordability, reachability and continuity. For a child, a school closed on the wrong day and open on the wrong day are both breaks in access.

Where this is headed

The indicators are new and no country has reported against them. The first district in India that publishes school attendance by heat day, measured at the school, will set the terms for everyone who follows, and the next revision of the state heat action plan will be written from what that district showed. A school trust gets to decide, before the next summer, whether it will be judged by its own reading or by a station nine kilometres away.
