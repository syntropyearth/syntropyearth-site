Thinking · Continuity

Two cyclones, one register. What Andhra Pradesh counted, and the list nobody keeps.

Abhijith Magal · 3 November 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR

  • Andhra Pradesh took two cyclones in six days: Arnab near Yanam on 28 October, Murasu near Ongole on 3 November 2026. Its health department published pre-landfall inventories that most states cannot match.
  • Before Arnab it identified 4,228 high-risk or near-term pregnant women and moved 3,100 to facilities; 367 delivered. Before Murasu it listed 6,870 women due between 1 and 10 November, stocked 43,672 anti-venom doses and readied 1,833 facilities with backup power.
  • Across six official reports and the minister's own order, the words dialysis, insulin, diabetes and hypertension do not appear once. The order of 26 October told village health workers to find "patients who require regular medication". No count followed, because no register holds them.
  • The maternity list exists because the antenatal system keeps one. The chronic-care list can be built the same way from registers that already exist. That is the Continuity Cohort Method, and an employer can run it for its own roster before the state does.

Credit first, because it is earned. In the week before Cyclone Arnab crossed the Andhra coast near Yanam in the early hours of 28 October 2026, the state's health department did something most Indian states have never done in public. It counted. On 27 October it reported 6,991 facilities on standby, 630 medical teams, 6,431 emergency beds, 716 vehicles and 13,101 anti-snake venom injections, and said that of 2,632 women due to deliver within two weeks, 1,075 had already been moved to hospital. Two days after landfall it published the outcome: 4,228 pregnant women identified as high-risk or near-term, 3,100 moved before the storm, 367 deliveries among them, 762 of the women in private hospitals, 64 hospitals and health centres damaged, 549 mobile medical units on standby, 50 camps run.

Then Murasu formed. Five days after Arnab, on a different stretch of the same coast, the department did it again, bigger. Before landfall near Ongole on the morning of 3 November it reported 1,688 medical teams, 116 relief centres, 13,165 emergency beds, 43,672 anti-venom doses, 733 ambulances, 721 mobile medical units, 300 maternity transport vehicles, 121 coastal health centres with emergency stocks, 1,833 health institutions with generators or alternative power, and 6,870 pregnant women due between 1 and 10 November, 493 of them already moved.

I have read every one of those reports, and the minister's order of 26 October that preceded them. The words dialysis, insulin, diabetes, hypertension and chronic appear in none of the inventories. Not once, across two storms, one state and nine days.

What did Andhra Pradesh's cyclone orders actually say about chronic patients?

More than most. On 26 October, Health Minister Satya Kumar Yadav directed that services at hospitals, health centres and village clinics run without interruption from 27 to 30 October, and named the groups that "must not face any disruption in healthcare": pregnant women, newborns, children, senior citizens and patients with chronic illnesses. Village health workers were told to identify vulnerable families, including those with "patients who require regular medication". The same order carried the number 26,768: women with expected delivery dates between 23 October and 6 November.

So the instruction existed. What never followed was a number. The maternity figure moved from 26,768 to 2,632 to 4,228 to 6,870 as the window and the districts changed, and each figure came with an action attached: identified, moved, delivered. The chronic-care instruction produced no figure at all. Not because the health workers ignored it. Because when a village worker is told to find "people needing regular medication" and the maternity worker is told to find "women due in the next ten days", only one of them has a register to open.

Why does the list exist for one group and not the other?

The antenatal system keeps a roster by design. Every registered pregnancy carries an expected delivery date in the state's maternal health records, which is why a district can produce 6,870 names sorted by due date inside a week. Maternity transport runs on that list in ordinary times. The cyclone plan simply read it under pressure.

Chronic care has no equivalent. A person on dialysis is on a unit's schedule, not a district's. A person on insulin is on a chemist's memory and a prescription in a drawer. A person on daily blood pressure tablets is in a non-communicable disease clinic register if they were screened, and in nobody's if they were diagnosed privately. The lists exist. They are scattered across institutions that have never been asked to produce them on the same day for the same purpose. "Find people needing regular medication" is an instruction to build a register from scratch, in seventy-two hours, during a cyclone warning.

There is a second absence. Every Arnab and Murasu report counted readiness before the storm. None reported continuity after it. We know 367 of the moved women delivered. We do not know how many of the thousands on dialysis in the Godavari districts missed a session while about 8,000 power poles lay down in Konaseema district, or how many insulin fridges went warm, or how many refills were missed across the 714 villages the NDRF counted as affected. Nobody was asked to count, so nobody did. When the Centre sent an eight-member team to assess Arnab's damage in Odisha from 2 to 4 November, it drew from Home, Agriculture, Finance, Water, Power, Roads, Rural Development and the space department. It had no one from health.

How would a chronic-care register be built before a storm?

The same way the maternity one was. Fix the list from registers that already exist, at the moment the warning is issued, and count it again after. The Continuity Cohort Method, which I published on Zenodo with a study protocol, does exactly this: a named cohort of people on continuous treatment is fixed from government and facility registers at the early action trigger, carried through the response, and reconciled at day twenty-one against the area's own baseline. Three fields make the roster usable in a cyclone: the condition and the treatment interval, the facility or chemist that supplies it, and a phone number that reaches the household. Dialysis rosters, NCD clinic registers and pharmacy refill logs already hold all three. The work is pulling them onto one page before the storm, not after.

A plan that names patients and never counts them is a promise without a receipt.

A state can do this, and Andhra Pradesh has shown it has the machinery. Its maternity system produced a list sorted by due date for two storms in a week. The question is whether the same instruction can reach a dialysis unit in Kakinada before the next warning. The gap is systemic, which Murasu proved: different districts, same silence.

What this means for an employer on the coast

An employer does not have to wait for the state. A company with a plant in Kakinada or a depot in Nellore holds a roster, an occupational health file and, for workers over 40 in specified sectors, the annual health examination the OSHWC Central Rules now require. Inside those records is the list the state could not produce: who on the payroll takes a daily medicine, who is on dialysis, who manages diabetes, and which chemist or clinic they depend on. It is a workforce continuity question before it is a public health one. A plant that loses its dialysis-dependent fitter for a fortnight after a storm has lost the fitter, and will not know why unless somebody counted.

I measure what heat does to the people a company depends on, at the sites where they work and learn, and I build the programme the company pays for to change that number. The same method runs on a storm as on a heat season: fix the cohort at the trigger, reach them before the event, count them at day twenty-one. The employer gets a continuity figure for its own people, the one number no cyclone plan in India has yet produced, and a BRSR Principle 3 disclosure with evidence behind it. The district gets a worked example it can copy.

Definition · climate-access

Climate-access is the gap between healthcare that exists on paper and healthcare people can actually reach once climate disruption is counted, read across four dimensions: availability, affordability, reachability and continuity. A cyclone breaks continuity first, and continuity is the dimension no inventory measured.

Where this is headed

Andhra Pradesh has now published pre-landfall health inventories for two cyclones in a week, which no other state has done, and its own order has already named the missing group. The distance between "find people needing regular medication" and a number is one register, built once and read under pressure. The first district that publishes a chronic-care count alongside its maternity count will set the standard for the coast, and the first employer that produces one for its own roster will have the proof point before the district does. The next warning is the deadline.
