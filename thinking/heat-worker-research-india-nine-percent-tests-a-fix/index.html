Thinking · Heat-Safe Workers

India writes more heat-and-worker research than any country on earth. Nine per cent of it tests a fix.

Abhijith Magal · 3 November 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

  • A Harvard team mapped 210 field studies of heat and workers in low- and middle-income countries. India supplied 63 of them, 30 per cent, more than any other country.
  • Only 9 per cent of the 210 tested an intervention. 39 per cent included no women. 10 per cent recorded whether the workers were migrants.
  • The research has measured heart rates in brick kilns and body temperatures on factory floors for decades. Almost none of it has asked whether a shade break, a water schedule or a blood pressure check would keep the same worker well through May.
  • That test cannot be run from a university. It needs a site, a roster and a season, which is what an employer already owns. The OSHWC Code duty gives the reason, and 193 billion lost labour hours a year gives the denominator.

India has studied heat and workers more than any country in the world. A mapping review from Harvard, published in Current Environmental Health Reports on 21 October 2026, screened 2,972 records and kept 210 field studies of occupational heat in low- and middle-income countries. Sixty-three of those came from India. Iran was next with 16 per cent, then China with 9. Manufacturing was the most studied sector at 36 per cent, agriculture, forestry and fishing next at 33. The earliest reports date to the 1960s.

Then the number that matters. Nine per cent of the 210 studies included an intervention to protect the workers they were measuring. Nineteen studies, across every poorer country on earth, across six decades. The other 191 took the reading and went home.

What has India's heat-and-worker research actually measured?

Physiology, mostly, and exposure. Core temperature in a kiln, heart rate on a construction site at 45 degrees, wet-bulb globe temperature on a shop floor in a Chennai summer, dehydration markers in cane cutters. The body of work is real and much of it is good. I have cited some of it in why the heat season is a healthcare operations problem. It tells us, with precision, that the worker is being harmed.

What it rarely tells us is whether anything changes the harm. A study that measures the heart rate of a loader at 2pm has answered the question a physiologist asks. It has not answered the one a plant head asks, which is whether moving the shift, adding a shade break at 11 and checking the blood pressure of the men over 40 would have kept that loader on the floor and out of the clinic. That second question is an intervention study, and intervention studies need a site that will change something and a roster that can be followed through a season.

Universities do not own sites. Employers do.

Why the gap sits with the employer, not the researcher

A heat-stress chamber in a laboratory can test a cooling vest. It cannot test a rest-shade-water schedule across a real May on a real floor with real piece-rate pressure, because the thing being tested is the site's own operating decisions. The only party that can run that experiment is the one that controls the shift pattern, the water points and the attendance register. That is not a complaint about researchers. It is a description of where the data lives.

Since 21 November 2025 the same party has carried a statutory duty to keep its workplace free from hazards likely to cause injury, with no heat threshold attached. I set out what that means in heat is now a workplace duty of care in India. A duty with no number gets judged after someone collapses, on the evidence the employer kept. An employer that has run and logged a tested heat protocol holds that evidence. One that funded a hydration drive and a poster does not.

And the denominator is now published. The Lancet Countdown's 2026 India data sheet puts potential labour hours lost to heat at 193 billion a year, averaged over 2016 to 2025, and earnings lost at USD 174 billion in 2025, 4.2 per cent of GDP. Agriculture carries 68 per cent of the hours. I read the full sheet in six India numbers, in operating terms. For an employer the arithmetic is short: a share of that loss is on its own roster, it has never been measured there, and the only experiment that would show what reduces it has been run nineteen times in sixty years.

The two groups the research left out

Thirty-nine per cent of the studies had no women in them. Ten per cent recorded whether the workers were migrants. The two omissions map onto the workforces that Indian employers depend on most and know least about.

Women are the majority of the agricultural workforce in large parts of the country and a growing share of garment and electronics assembly. The heat physiology literature is built mostly on men. A company that tests a protocol on a mixed floor and records the result by sex is producing evidence that does not currently exist.

Migrant workers are the construction and seasonal agricultural workforce, often on a different contractor's roster, often not acclimatised, often the first to disappear from the attendance register in May without anyone recording why. A site that cannot say what share of its workers arrived this season cannot design an acclimatisation schedule, and acclimatisation is the one heat intervention with the strongest evidence behind it.

What a site study looks like, with the systems a site already runs

Nothing in this needs new machinery. The attendance register already exists. The contractor's muster roll already exists. The occupational health file for workers over 40, which the Central Rules of 8 May 2026 now require in specified sectors, already exists or is about to. The site has a forecast and, in most states, a heat action plan with colour codes. A wet-bulb globe thermometer costs less than a day's canteen bill.

The study is the act of reading those together. Measure heat where the work happens, not at the airport station. Fix the roster at the start of the season: who is on it, by sex, by arrival date, by whether they take a daily medicine for blood pressure or diabetes. Change one thing at a defined trigger and write down what changed. Then count, at the end of the season, what moved: heat incidents, absence by heatwave week, output, and whether the workers on treatment were still on it in June. That last count is the Continuity Cohort Method applied to a workforce, and it is the piece nobody in the 210 studies attempted.

We can measure a kiln worker's heart rate at 45 degrees to the decimal. We have almost never tested whether a shade break would have kept him working and well through May.

This is the work I do. I measure what heat does to the people a company depends on, at the sites where they work and learn, and I build the programme the company pays for to change that number. Heat-Safe Workers is a season, not a camp: one site, one roster, one summer, measured before and after. The employer gets a logged protocol that answers the OSHWC Code's general duty, a BRSR Principle 3 disclosure with data behind it, and a result that belongs in the 9 per cent.

Definition · climate-access

Climate-access is the gap between healthcare that exists on paper and healthcare people can actually reach once climate disruption is counted, read across four dimensions: availability, affordability, reachability and continuity. For a workforce, the first break is usually reachability on the hot afternoon itself.

Where this is headed

The next edition of a review like this one will count India's share of the world's heat-and-worker research again, and it will probably be higher. Whether the intervention share moves off 9 per cent depends on whether sites run tests this coming summer. The researchers who wrote the review have worked on heat in India for years and would, I suspect, be glad of a site willing to be studied. The window is the one between now and March, while it is cool enough to design and too early to be judged.
