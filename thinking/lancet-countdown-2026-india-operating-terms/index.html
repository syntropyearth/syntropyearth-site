The Lancet Countdown 2026, in operating terms. Six India numbers, and the one the sheet does not carry.

Abhijith Magal · 3 November 2026 · 9 min read

TL;DR

  • The 2026 India data sheet, dated 23 October 2026, puts heat's bill at USD 174 billion in lost earnings in 2025, 4.2 per cent of GDP, against 0.91 per cent for the world.
  • 193 billion potential labour hours a year are lost to heat, 75 per cent above the 1990s. Agriculture carries 68 per cent of the hours and 54 per cent of the earnings. The site, not the country, is where that lands.
  • Indians now spend over 2,600 hours a year at moderate-or-higher heat-stress risk during a brisk walk, and lose 64 hours of sleep a year to hot nights. Each is a site condition before it is a health statistic.
  • The sheet carries no adaptation indicator: no line on plans, vulnerability assessments or who stayed on treatment. That is the number a company or a school has to produce for itself, and the 2025 reading still stands behind it.

Last year I read the 2025 India data sheet as five numbers in operating terms. The 2026 sheet arrived on the India country page on 23 October, five days before the global report launched in London on 28 October, and almost nobody noticed because the main country-resources page did not list it. It changes the reading in two ways. The money number is now expressed as a share of GDP, which is the unit a board actually hears. And the sheet has dropped any pretence of measuring adaptation. Every indicator on it measures exposure or cost. Not one measures what anybody did about it.

So here are the six numbers that carry the operating weight this year, converted for the people who run a plant, a depot, a school or a CSR budget, and then the line that is missing.

What does the Lancet Countdown 2026 say about India?

That heat cost India USD 174 billion in lost earnings in 2025, 4.2 per cent of GDP against a global figure of 0.91 per cent. That the country loses 193 billion potential labour hours a year to heat, 75 per cent more than the 1990s average, with agriculture carrying 68 per cent of the hours. That 5.3 of the 10.6 heatwave days an average Indian lived through each year between 2016 and 2025 would not have happened without climate change. That each person now spends over 2,600 hours a year at moderate-or-higher heat-stress risk during a brisk walk, and loses about 64 hours of sleep a year to hot nights. And that net fossil fuel subsidies of USD 62.3 billion in 2024 equalled about 49 per cent of what the country spends on health.

4.2 per cent of GDP: the number that ends the materiality argument

Last year the sheet gave USD 194 billion for 2024. This year it gives USD 174 billion for 2025 and adds the denominator. A smaller dollar figure and a share of GDP that is four and a half times the world's. The second framing is the one to carry into a boardroom, because a finance director does not hold the GDP of India in their head and does hold a ratio. Heat removes a twenty-fourth of national output in a year. The share for a company whose workforce stands outdoors in Vidarbha or on a shop floor in Coimbatore is not 4.2 per cent. It is whatever the site's own roster says it is, and that number is the one almost no company has.

Use the national figure to open the conversation. Do not use it to answer the question that follows, which is always "and what is ours?"

193 billion hours, 68 per cent in agriculture: the site is the unit

The hours number has moved from a single reporting year to a ten-year average, which is the better statistic and the less dramatic one. What matters more is the split. Agriculture carries 68 per cent of the lost hours and 54 per cent of the lost earnings. Everything a food, beverage, textile or agri-input company depends on in the first mile of its supply chain is inside that 68 per cent.

The conversion is a question a procurement head can answer in an afternoon. Which of our sourcing districts sat inside the heatwave days last April and May, and what did arrivals do in those weeks? The sheet proves the pressure. The arrivals ledger shows where it landed. A company that reads the two together has a Heat-Safe Workers case before anyone has designed a programme.

5.3 of 10.6 days: the attributable share has held at half

Last year's sheet said 6.6 of 19.8 days in 2024, a third. This year's gives a ten-year average: 5.3 of 10.6 heatwave days between 2016 and 2025, half. Two different windows, so the two figures do not contradict each other, and the ten-year version is the sturdier one for an internal case. "We have always had hot summers" is still the sentence that kills climate-health proposals in meetings. Half of the heatwave days a worker has faced for a decade would not have happened without warming. That is the reply.

2,600 hours and 64 hours of sleep: conditions, before they are statistics

Two indicators on the sheet are site conditions dressed as national averages. The first says a person walking briskly in India faces moderate-or-higher heat-stress risk for over 2,600 hours a year, more than 200 above the 1990s. A brisk walk is the exertion level of a loader, a line worker on a hot floor, a child crossing a playground in May. The second says hot nights now cost each person about 64 hours of sleep a year. A worker who slept badly all week is not the same worker on Friday's shift, and the heat-stress curves used to set rest cycles assume rested bodies.

Neither number is measured at any site. Each could be. A wet-bulb globe thermometer at the hottest workstation and a night-time reading in the workers' housing are the cheapest instruments a programme buys. The argument for measuring where people actually stand is made in measure heat where the child stands, and it applies to a factory gate as much as a school gate.

49 per cent: the subsidy number nobody will quote

Net fossil fuel subsidies of USD 62.3 billion in 2024, about 49 per cent of national health expenditure, and more than the country collects in carbon pricing revenue. I include it not because a company can act on it but because a CSR committee will hear it in the next twelve months, probably from a journalist, and should know where it comes from. It is indicator 4.3.2, it is a net figure, and it is the sharpest single sentence in the sheet for anyone who controls money.

What the sheet does not count

Nothing on the 2026 India sheet is an adaptation indicator. There is no line for whether a health adaptation plan exists, no vulnerability assessment, no climate-informed surveillance, no count of anyone kept on treatment through a heat month. The global report, by contrast, does carry an adaptation ledger: 71 per cent of WHO member states had no plan to adapt their health systems by 2025, and climate-informed early warning exists in 75 countries. India sits on the right side of that 71 per cent. It has a national programme on climate change and human health, and most states carry a heat action plan. Which makes the question for an Indian site sharper, not softer. The plan exists. Does it reach the person on the roster?

The sheet measures what heat does to the country. Nobody measures what it does to a site, because nobody is asked to.

This is the gap the practice is built around, and it is a measurement job before it is a programme job. I measure what heat does to the people a company depends on, at the sites where they work and learn, and I build the programme the company pays for to change that number. The measurement side borrows the sheet's own logic and brings it down to one roster: heat-stress hours at the workstation, absence and output by heatwave week, workers on daily treatment for blood pressure or diabetes and whether their refills held through May. The Continuity Cohort Method fixes that last group as a named cohort and counts them again after the season. None of that is new machinery. It is the attendance register, the occupational health file and the site thermometer, read together for the first time.

Definition · climate-access

Climate-access is the gap between healthcare that exists on paper and healthcare people can actually reach once climate disruption is counted, read across four dimensions: availability, affordability, reachability and continuity. The 2026 sheet measures none of the four.

Four things to do with the 2026 numbers before March

Replace last year's dollar figure on slide three with 4.2 per cent of GDP, and put the world's 0.91 next to it. Pull last summer's attendance, output or arrivals data by week, lay the district's heatwave days over it, and see where the two collide. Put a wet-bulb globe thermometer at the hottest workstation on the hottest site and start a log, because the duty of care already exists under the OSHWC Code and the evidence does not. And for anyone on the roster who takes a daily medicine, decide now who will count whether they were still on it in June.

The fair objection

These are national averages, and a sheet that covers Leh and Nellore describes neither. Correct. The objection is a reason to localise, not to file the sheet. The Countdown tells a company the pressure is real, rising, and now expressed in the unit its board uses. Only the site's own data can say where it is landing. The next edition will carry the same gap unless somebody starts producing the missing line, and the sites that produce it first will be the ones whose numbers end up quoted.

  • India lost 193 billion potential labour hours a year to heat between 2016 and 2025, 75 per cent above the 1990s average; agriculture accounted for about 68 per cent.
