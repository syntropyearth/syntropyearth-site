20 heat deaths or 359? The alert that never reached the site.

Abhijith Magal · 3 November 2026 · 7 min read

TL;DR

  • HeatWatch India tracked ten sets of news outlets from March to July 2026 and logged 359 heat-related deaths, 86 confirmed and 273 suspected, across 17 states and Delhi. The National Centre for Disease Control confirmed 20 over a similar period.
  • The gap is a counting gap first. Only 23.4 per cent of deaths in India are medically certified, four agencies use different definitions, and HeatWatch could find 63 public cooling centres in the whole country.
  • The alert gap is the one an employer owns. IMD says its warnings reach 80 per cent of the population. In a survey of construction workers in Raipur, 13.6 per cent knew of any heat alert.
  • A warning that reaches the district and not the gate has not been delivered. A site that logs when the alert arrived, what changed, and who was affected is producing the record the national count cannot.

Twenty, or 359. Those are the two counts of India's heat deaths in the summer of 2026, and the distance between them is the subject of a stocktake HeatWatch India published on 28 October. The 359 comes from tracking English and nine Indian-language news outlets from March to July: 86 deaths confirmed by an official or a doctor, 273 suspected, across 17 states and Delhi. The 20 is what the National Centre for Disease Control confirmed over a similar period. Neither is the true number. One is what the newspapers saw; the other is what the surveillance system was able to certify. The true number is higher than both, and nobody in India can say by how much.

I wrote in Europe counted its heat dead in season that India already collects the registry and weather data a modelled count would need. HeatWatch's report shows why the direct count fails before the modelling even starts. Only 23.4 per cent of deaths in India are medically certified. Four agencies carry four definitions of a heat death. A worker who collapses on a site in Raipur and dies in a private hospital that evening is, in most states, a cardiac arrest.

Why does the official count stay so low?

Because a heat death has to pass three gates to be counted, and most fall at the first. The person has to die somewhere a doctor certifies the cause. The doctor has to write heat, not the organ that failed. And the certificate has to reach a surveillance system that uses the same definition as the state next door. HeatWatch's recommendations follow from that chain: a humidity-adjusted warning measure, wet-bulb globe temperature for one, all-cause excess mortality as the primary yardstick, one common death definition with public district-level data, a heat-stroke-ready centre in every district, cooling centres listed publicly and open at night, and heatstroke made a notifiable condition.

Every one of those is a state or central decision. A company cannot make heatstroke notifiable. What a company can do is the thing the report's most uncomfortable number points at.

Who actually hears the heat alert?

IMD says its alerts reach 80 per cent of India's population. That figure is about broadcast: the warning is issued, carried by the media, pushed to phones. HeatWatch surveyed construction workers in Raipur and found that 13.6 per cent were aware of any heat alert. The two numbers can be true at once. The alert reached the district. It did not reach the man carrying bricks on the fourth floor, who does not watch the news at noon, whose phone is in a bag at the gate, and whose contractor was not asked to tell him.

That gap is not a weather service failure. The weather service delivered. The gap sits between the district and the gate, and the only party standing in it is the employer. The OSHWC Code's general duty, in force since 21 November 2025, asks employers to keep the workplace free from hazards likely to cause injury, and sets no heat threshold. An inspector asking after a collapse will want to know what the site did when the warning was issued. "The warning was issued" is not an answer. "The warning reached the site at 7.40am, the shift was shortened at 11, and the names of the workers over 40 on blood pressure medication were checked by the supervisor" is.

What a site does with a warning system that stops at the district

Finish the delivery. The alert already exists; the site's job is the last hundred metres. Write down who receives the IMD and state heat action plan warning each morning, and what they do with it: which supervisor, which contractor, which language on the board at the gate. Tie actions to the colour code the state already uses, so the site is not inventing thresholds. Keep a log that records the time the warning arrived and the time the shift changed. And keep a list of the workers for whom a heat day is a medical day, which the annual health examination for workers over 40 under the Central Rules of 8 May 2026 now gives most sites a reason to hold.

Then count. The reason HeatWatch had to read newspapers is that nobody holds a site-level record of what heat did. A site that logs heat incidents as safety incidents, records absence by heatwave week and checks at the end of the season whether its workers on daily treatment were still on it, has a number the national surveillance system cannot produce, and a BRSR Principle 3 disclosure with data behind it. I measure what heat does to the people a company depends on, at the sites where they work and learn, and I build the programme the company pays for to change that number. The alert-reach figure is the first thing a Heat-Safe Workers season measures, because it is the cheapest to move and the one an inspector will ask about first.

A warning that reaches the district and not the gate has not been delivered. The last hundred metres belong to the employer.

Definition · climate-access

Climate-access is the gap between healthcare that exists on paper and healthcare people can actually reach once climate disruption is counted, read across four dimensions: availability, affordability, reachability and continuity. A warning nobody on the site heard is a reachability failure before the first collapse.

The fair caveat on the numbers

HeatWatch's 359 is a media count, and media counts over-report in some districts and miss others entirely. The Raipur survey is one city and one trade, and the report does not give a sample size on its public page. Neither weakens the point. A funder who sees "20 deaths" sees no problem, and a site that assumes the 80 per cent figure describes its own workers has not checked. Either is checkable by a company for its own roster this coming summer, and the check costs a clipboard.

Where this is headed

HeatWatch has asked for heatstroke to be made notifiable and for excess mortality to become the national yardstick. If either happens, the official count will jump, and the first question asked of large employers will be what their sites were doing on the days the count rose. The sites with a log will answer in an afternoon. The rest will be reading the newspaper count and wondering whether their own names are in it.
