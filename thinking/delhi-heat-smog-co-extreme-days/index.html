Thinking · Evidence

Heat and smog used to take turns in Delhi. On 388 days they arrived together.

Abhijith Magal · 3 November 2026 · 7 min read

TL;DR

  • A Hong Kong Baptist University team used satellite and ground monitoring data from 2017 to 2024 to count 388 days in Delhi when extreme heat and severe pollution coincided. In April 2022 it was more than half of all days. The count was falling before 2020 and has climbed since.
  • Heat is part of the cause. Hot, still air speeds up the chemistry that forms fine organic carbon particles, which rose to as much as 2.3 times their usual level during extreme heat.
  • The heat action plan and the clean air plan are written by different departments on different calendars. A worker with asthma on a 44 degree afternoon in air you can taste has one set of lungs and one day.
  • Delhi's AQI went from 117 on 31 October to 327 on 2 November 2026 and GRAP Stage II began with no health instruction attached. A site needs its own trigger for the day that is both, and the inputs already exist.

Until this autumn my heat argument and my air argument were two separate conversations, held with two separate sets of people. A study reported on 19 October 2026 joins them. Huibin Dai and colleagues at Hong Kong Baptist University, publishing in Advances in Atmospheric Sciences, used satellite and ground monitoring data from 2017 to 2024 to ask a simple question about India's pre-monsoon season: what does extreme heat do to the air? The answer is that hot, still air speeds up the chemistry that turns gases into fine particles. During extreme heat, concentrations of fine organic carbon rose to as much as 2.3 times their usual levels. Heat is not just arriving alongside pollution. It is manufacturing some of it.

Then the count. Delhi had 388 days between 2017 and 2024 on which extreme heat and severe pollution occurred together. In April 2022, more than half of all days qualified. The number of these co-extreme days was falling before 2020 and has risen since.

What does a co-extreme day do to a workforce?

The study measures air chemistry, not health outcomes. The authors say quantifying the health impact is their next step, and I will not put numbers in their mouths. What I can say is what the two hazards each do on their own, because that evidence is settled, and what it means when they land on the same afternoon.

Heat raises the cost of every breath and every heartbeat. A worker at moderate exertion in 44 degrees is already near the limit of what the body can shed. Fine particles inflame airways and load the cardiovascular system, which is why severe pollution episodes fill respiratory and cardiac wards. The person who carries the two at once is the one with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure or uncontrolled hypertension, and that person is on most rosters in Delhi, usually unidentified. I set out in air quality is an access problem too how severe episodes also stop routine care: the clinic visit postponed, the inhaler refill skipped because nobody wants to travel through it. Add heat and the postponement gets longer.

Then there is the simpler operational fact. On a heat day a site moves work earlier, into the cool of the morning. On a smog day the worst air often sits in exactly that early window, before the mixing layer lifts. The two standard responses contradict each other, and nobody has told the supervisor which one wins.

Why does nobody own the day that is both?

Because the plans are written by different people. Delhi's heat action plan belongs to the disaster management side and runs from March. The Graded Response Action Plan belongs to the Commission for Air Quality Management and runs from October. On 2 November 2026, with the AQI at 327 after a 210-point rise in three days, CAQM invoked Stage II: coal and firewood banned, diesel generators restricted, parking fees raised, mechanical sweeping stepped up. The order carried no health instruction. No advisory for employers, no line on who should stay indoors, nothing about the patient whose inhaler was running low.

That is not a criticism of CAQM, whose remit is emissions. It is the gap. The study says the two seasons now overlap in April and May, when heat is making the air worse. The institutional calendar still treats them as a relay, with the baton passed in September.

What does a site do with this?

Build one trigger from two inputs it already receives. The IMD heat warning and the CPCB air quality index arrive on a phone together before 8am. A co-extreme day is one on which the two cross the site's own thresholds, and a site can define that line this month. On such a day the actions are specific and cheap. Workers with a known respiratory or cardiac condition are on a list, and somebody checks them. Inhaler and cardiac medicine stock is held at the site clinic or the nearest chemist, because the afternoon on which a refill gets missed is the one on which the air is worst. Shift timing is decided from the two readings together, not the forecast alone. And the day is logged, so that at the end of the season the site can see what co-extreme days did to attendance, incidents and refills.

The list of workers with a heat-sensitive or air-sensitive condition is the part most sites do not have, and it is the part that matters. The annual health examination now required for workers over 40 in specified sectors under the OSHWC Central Rules is where it starts, and the Continuity Cohort Method is how it gets counted through a season. I measure what heat does to the people a company depends on, at the sites where they work and learn, and I build the programme the company pays for to change that number. A co-extreme trigger is a design element inside a Heat-Safe Workers season for any site in the Indo-Gangetic plain, not a separate programme.

The heat plan and the clean air plan are run by different people. The worker's lungs only have one day.

Definition · climate-access

Climate-access is the gap between healthcare that exists on paper and healthcare people can actually reach once climate disruption is counted, read across four dimensions: availability, affordability, reachability and continuity. A co-extreme day breaks reachability and continuity at once.

The honest caveats

Three. The study measures chemistry, not admissions, and its authors say so. The 388 figure is Delhi's, and the mechanism will play differently in Mumbai's sea breeze and Lucknow's winter inversions. And the organic carbon finding describes a fraction of fine particulate matter, not the whole of it, so "2.3 times" is not "the air was 2.3 times worse". None of that changes what a site should do. A trigger built from two public readings costs nothing to set and a season to test, and the season that tests it is the one starting in March.

Where this is headed

The study's own next step is the health impact, and when that paper lands the co-extreme day will have a mortality and admissions figure attached. The sites that logged their own co-extreme days by then will be able to read it against their attendance records. The ones that did not will read it in the newspaper, in the first week of April, with the forecast already red.
