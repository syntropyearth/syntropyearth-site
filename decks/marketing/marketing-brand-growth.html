03 · AREA TWO · OPERATIONS AND COMMERCIAL

Useful where a good strategy is being lost somewhere between approval and execution.

Deliverable
What it contains
Process and cost review
Where effort, spend and cycle time are actually going, mapped against what the operation is meant to produce. The diagnostic that precedes any redesign worth doing.
Operating-model design
Role and decision architecture, ways of working across markets and functions, and the governance that keeps a distributed team working to one standard rather than eleven local ones.

The financial model showing what each channel, segment or market genuinely costs to serve, and what changes under different scenarios. The artefact that turns an operating opinion into a board conversation.

Support through GCC design and stand-up, including which work genuinely belongs there, how quality is held at distance, and how the centre earns scope rather than being handed it.

Every deliverable here ends in a number a finance team can check, not a recommendation a finance team has to take on faith.

04 · HOW THE WORK IS BUILT

Three things that make the difference between a plan and a plan that survives.

01

Written for execution, not approval

The test of a deliverable here is whether a team can pick it up and work from it in week one, not whether it presented well in the meeting where it was signed off. Those are different documents, and most consultancies produce the second one.

02

The operating detail is included, not scoped out

Sales-force effectiveness, cost models, governance and review rhythm sit inside the work rather than being the follow-on project nobody funds. That detail is usually where a strategy quietly fails.

03

One operator, not a pyramid

The person in the room is the person doing the work. No engagement team where the experience you bought appears at the kickoff and the readout, and juniors fill the twelve weeks in between.

Plans that survive contact with a sales force tend to be written by people who have had to stand in front of one.

That is the method. The commercial terms behind it are deliberately simple.

05 · HOW ENGAGEMENTS RUN

Two shapes. Both start with a named deliverable.

Engagements are scoped as projects with a fixed figure and a named deliverable, or as a retainer where the work is continuous.

SHAPE A

Project

A fixed figure against a named deliverable and a date. Most first engagements run this way, because it lets the work prove itself before either side commits to anything larger. You know what arrives and when before you sign.

SHAPE B

Retainer

Where the work is genuinely continuous rather than a defined piece with an end, an ongoing advisory arrangement scoped to a set of responsibilities and a rhythm. Usually follows a project rather than opening the relationship.

Scope moves before the rate does.

If the budget does not reach the work, the honest answer is a smaller, sharper piece of work at the same standard, not the same scope delivered thinner. Pricing is shared in the proposal, sized to what is actually being asked for.

Clear commercial terms are part of the deliverable, not a negotiation to get through first.

06 · WHERE THIS STOPS

Not an agency, and it says so before you find out the hard way.

This is strategy, operating design and orchestration. It sits alongside your agencies and your internal team rather than replacing either.

  • No creative production, media buying or campaign execution. That is what the roster is for, and orchestrating it well requires not competing with it.
  • No regulatory or medical-legal review. Scientific and medical content is developed with specialised medical partners and goes through your own review process, where it belongs.
  • No day-to-day brand management or interim staffing. The goal is a plan and an operating standard your team runs, not a dependency on an outside adviser.
  • No promise to be permanent. A good engagement ends with your team more capable than it was, and a clear reason if it continues.

Where specialist or delivery partners are needed, we can help identify them and hold them to the standard the work requires.
