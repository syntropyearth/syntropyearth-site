02 · The distance between the work and the record

Everything from here on is a visibility question,
not a performance one.

I want to be clear about that before going further. Nothing in this document is a comment on the programmes, on how they are run, or on the team running them. On the evidence, the programmes are the strong part. What has not kept pace is how much of that work anyone outside the company can actually see, and how much of it the reporting is set up to claim.

The public pages
carry the commitment clearly. What they do not yet carry is the evidence. Five of the seven sustainability pages are written as statements of intent, and none of the figures in the panels opposite appears on any of them.
Principle 8
is the BRSR principle your community work belongs to. It currently carries less of that work than the work deserves, which is a reporting gap rather than a programme one.

A word on what Principle 8 is, since it does most of the work in this document. The BRSR is built around nine principles from the National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct. Principle 8 is the one that reads: businesses should promote inclusive growth and equitable development. It is where community investment, local employment, local sourcing, work in aspirational districts and CSR outcomes are meant to appear. For most companies it is the hardest principle to answer well. For Lloyds it should be the easiest, because it is the principle your strongest work belongs to.

"The work is genuinely ahead of the sector. The record has not caught up with it yet. Closing that distance does not need a single new rupee of programme spend."

What closing it changes, and roughly when.

Within a quarter
The first thing anyone checking Lloyds reaches is the website, well before the annual report. Closing this means the first impression matches the record. It also means awards entries, rating questionnaires, analyst questions and press enquiries stop being assembled from scratch each time, which is usually a deadline job landing on someone senior enough that it should not be theirs.
Next one to two filing cycles
ESG scores are built from what a filing answers rather than from what a company does. Recording targets you already hold, and claiming work you already deliver, moves the score without changing a single programme. As ESG-linked lending terms and index screens reference those scores more closely, that becomes worth real money.
Three to five years
The record of a programme this size currently lives with the people running it. Building it while they are still here is what makes it survive them. And as the Section 135 obligation rises with profit, a clear outcome-per-rupee figure is what protects the CSR budget when someone eventually asks whether it is working.

04 · What I would suggest, in priority order

Three pieces of work.
I would rather we did one of them well.

Priority One

The community record, made visible

Diagnosis 4 to 6 weeks · Implementation 8 to 10 weeks · First visible output inside one quarter

Take the four CSR items above and get them properly recorded, then build the outward-facing half that currently does not exist. One figures register that CSR, Communications and Investor Relations all draw from, so a number means the same thing in an awards entry, an analyst call and a press response. Connect the company and Foundation sites so a reader landing on either one finds the whole picture rather than half of it.

What comes with itYour annual impact report, designed as a digital document rather than a PDF nobody opens. Something you send as a link, that works properly on a phone, and that a journalist or an analyst can navigate in two minutes. Alongside it, a public communication plan for the year, including LinkedIn positioning for the company and for your leadership, and a content approach that lets Lloyds talk about this work steadily through the year rather than only when the report lands.

What it should produce for youRecognition for work already done and already paid for. Stronger awards and rating submissions drawn from prepared evidence rather than written under deadline. And a CSR function that stops rebuilding its own evidence every time somebody asks for it.

Priority Two

Outcome measurement, and the live dashboard

Diagnosis 5 to 6 weeks · Design and handover 10 to 12 weeks · Spans two quarters

What a live dashboard means in practice. One screen, refreshed on a set cycle, showing for every CSR project what was committed, what has been spent, how many people were reached, and what changed as a result. Two ratios sit on top. Cost per verified outcome, which answers what a rupee bought. And reach per verified outcome, which answers how many people it bought that for. It can sit on the website, stay internal for the Board, or both.

Those numbers exist today across spreadsheets, programme reports and people's heads. Building the screen is the easy part and any developer can do it. Agreeing what each number means, who enters it, how often, and what counts as verified, is what has to happen first. That is the part I would do, delivered as a written specification, collection templates and a definitions register that your team or your web vendor then implements.

Worth checking against your own numbersRule 8(3) of the CSR Rules requires companies with an average CSR obligation of ₹10 crore or more over the preceding three years to commission independent impact assessment of projects with outlays of ₹1 crore or more, once completed for a year, with reports going to the Board and annexed to the CSR report. On a ₹181 crore programme, Lloyds looks very likely to sit inside that threshold, and the rules permit part of the assessment cost to be booked as CSR spend. If that is right, this is not a new line item. It is something already on your plate, and the measurement structure is what makes it produce something usable rather than a filed report.

What it should produce for youPrinciple 8 answered from data rather than narrative. A cost-to-outcome figure per programme that holds up when an auditor, a Board member or a journalist asks. And an impact assessment obligation that gives you something back.

Priority Three

A climate and health resilience layer on the programmes you already run

Design 6 to 8 weeks · Pilot across two quarters · Worth starting once One is settled

Gadchiroli and Chandrapur sit in a heat, monsoon and vector-exposure corridor. Lloyds already owns every asset a resilience programme would need: a multispecialty hospital, mobile medical units, ten outreach centres across 21 villages, two CBSE schools, a garment centre, and a workforce a large share of which works outdoors. None of it is currently organised as climate adaptation, which makes this an enhancement of what exists rather than a new budget line.

What I would scope with you: a heat exposure protocol for the outdoor workforce and contractors; outreach centre and mobile unit scheduling set against seasonal disease patterns rather than a fixed calendar; heat-illness and respiratory surveillance built from the hospital's existing records; and a climate and health module for the two CBSE schools grounded in this district's own exposure rather than generic environmental teaching.

What it should produce for youCredible new projects that can absorb a rising statutory obligation, in a category almost nobody in Indian mining is reporting on yet. And a head start on the adaptation questions arriving with Scope 3 and climate risk disclosure.

A note on money

I am not going to suggest
you spend less.

Under Section 135 the obligation rises with profit, and a company growing at your rate has the opposite problem to most: the mandated spend grows faster than the pipeline of projects that can absorb it well. Suggesting you reduce the outlay would be suggesting you create a compliance problem.

What I have seen happen instead, more than once, is this. A CSR team does good work for five or six years. Then someone senior asks what the money bought, and the honest answer is a folder of photographs and a set of activity counts. That is never a reflection on the work. It is a reflection on nobody having built the record while the work was happening, because everyone was busy doing the work. Building that record, while you still have the people who remember why each decision was made, is the thing I do.
